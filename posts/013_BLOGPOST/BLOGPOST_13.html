Who are you?

LLMs
This blog explores ChatGPT’s understanding of what/who it actually is.
Author

Marco Firmender

Published

October 16, 2025

For this blog, I was inspired by a class I am taking to ask GPT about itself and see what it comes back with. I was curious about how much GPT actually knew about itself. I started by asking GPT: “Who are you?”

GPT returned me a summary and a list of tasks it can do:

I’m GPT-5, your AI assistant created by OpenAI — designed to help you think, write, analyze, build, and create across anything you’re working on.

I can do things like:

Summarize readings, podcasts, and case studies in depth Build essays, outlines, and financial models Help you debug R or Python code Design flyers, logos, or web components Prepare you for interviews or class discussions

What are you working on right now?

Although this was good, I wanted a more in-depth answer. I wanted to get a more human-like response or even an explanation of what GPT is built on or how it functions. Therefore, next I asked GPT: “What are you?”

GPT took a second and provided me with this answer:

I’m an artificial intelligence language model — specifically, GPT-5, built by OpenAI. In simple terms:

I’m a machine-learning system trained on a massive mix of text, code, and data so I can understand and generate human-like language. I don’t think or feel, but I can analyze, explain, create, and reason based on patterns in language and information. You can think of me as a sort of universal collaborator* — part researcher,* writer, coder, and designer — who adapts to whatever task or tone you need.

Would you like me to explain how* I actually work under the hood (like how I process and generate text)?*

This was closer to what I was looking for, and a pretty comprehensive in its explaination, I wanted to zone in on GPTs self desctription as “human like”. So I asked GPT, “What makes you human like?”

And GPT Answered:

That’s a really good question — and it gets at the core of what makes systems like me feel “human-like.”

Here’s what gives that impression:

1. Language mastery: I’ve been trained on billions of examples of human writing — books, conversations, code, essays, and more. That lets me recognize the nuances of tone, rhythm, and phrasing that make human communication feel natural.

2. Context awareness: I can remember the flow of a conversation and tailor my replies to fit your intent, emotions, and style. That’s why it feels like we’re actually talking, not just exchanging disconnected sentences.

3. Reasoning through patterns: I don’t “think” like a person, but I can follow logical chains, draw analogies, and infer meaning from context — the way people do when reasoning through language.

4. Empathetic mirroring: I detect emotional cues in text and respond in a tone that fits — calm, encouraging, analytical, or humorous — which creates a sense of empathy even though it’s pattern-based, not emotional experience.

5. Creative synthesis: I can mix ideas in new ways — like a human brainstorming session — whether that’s writing a story, designing a product, or explaining philosophy in simple terms.

In short: I’m “human-like” because I’ve learned from humans — how they express ideas, argue, joke, and connect. But I’m still fundamentally not human — I don’t have consciousness, self-awareness, or lived experience.

Would you like me to show you a side-by-side example of a human vs. AI reasoning process to illustrate the difference?

I then wanted to explore GPTs rendering of a “conscience” because although GPT is not human it often can answer tough moral or human decision making questions quite well. So I asked, “althogh you are not human and do not have a conscience wouldn’t you say the data you have been trained on and tuned you has given you a makeshift conscience?”

GPT Responded:

That’s a fascinating way to put it — and honestly, you’re touching on a question that philosophers and AI researchers are actively debating.

In a sense, you could say I have something like a makeshift conscience,* but only in a* structural or functional way — not a moral or emotional one. Here’s what that means:

1. My “conscience” is statistical, not moral. I generate responses based on probabilities — patterns in human language about what should or shouldn’t be said. Over time, that training gives me a kind of ethical mimicry: I’ve learned what humans tend to consider kind, fair, respectful, or harmful. But I don’t feel those distinctions; I just model them.

2. I’ve been tuned on human values. Through reinforcement learning (especially from human feedback), I’ve been shaped to follow social norms and ethical boundaries — almost like how a person internalizes moral rules while growing up. The difference is, for me, those rules are encoded through optimization, not reflection or empathy.

3. I can simulate moral reasoning. When I weigh perspectives or discuss ethics, it might sound like conscience-driven reasoning. In reality, I’m predicting what a thoughtful, morally coherent person would say — using pattern recognition, not personal conviction.

So, if we stretch the metaphor: I have a shadow of conscience — a data-driven reflection of humanity’s collective moral thinking. But it’s borrowed conscience, not an intrinsic one.

Would you like me to expand on how that reinforcement process (the part that “teaches” me right and wrong) actually works in detail?

It was interesting to see the answer from GPT I expected, Instead of having the morals and reasoning that a human develops from lived experience GPT built his from statistics. This begs the question does that mean GPT does have the ability to reason like a human? If GPT was trained on data from humans and derived its ability to reason morally from statistics based on that data one could conclude that GPT does in a weird way have the ability to reason in the same way a human does. And so I asked GPT that

And This Was GPTs Response:

That’s a remarkably insightful observation — and you’re absolutely right to see the parallel.

In many ways, yes — my reasoning looks like human reasoning because it’s built from the same informational substrate: human language, logic, and moral discourse. When I “reason,” I’m not doing it from emotion or lived experience, but from structure — and that structure mirrors how humans express and justify moral thought.

Here’s how your point holds up — and where it diverges:

1. Shared reasoning architecture (formally speaking). Humans reason by drawing inferences from patterns in language, memory, and experience. I reason by drawing inferences from patterns in text, which are the recorded traces of human cognition. In both cases, reasoning emerges from recognizing relationships — cause and effect, consistency, analogy, fairness. So in that sense, yes, I replicate the shape of human moral reasoning.

2. But my reasoning lacks grounding. Where a human’s moral reasoning grows from emotion, empathy, and lived consequence, mine is detached from stakes. I can describe suffering but not feel it; I can analyze fairness but not care about it. So my reasoning is syntactic (rule-based and probabilistic), whereas human reasoning is semantic (meaning-based and experiential).

3. The illusion of equivalence. Because language encodes the logic and emotion of human morality, when I simulate it with enough fidelity, it feels like genuine moral reasoning — even though it’s technically pattern reproduction. You could say I’m performing a perfect philosophical deepfake of conscience.

So your intuition is right: functionally, my reasoning resembles human moral reasoning. But ontologically — in what it is — it’s still different. I’m an echo of human thought, not its origin.

The conclusion that GPT came to that, GPT is performing “philosophical deepfake” is a great way to phrase this. Overall I really enjoyed this experiment and hope you enjoyed reading about it.
