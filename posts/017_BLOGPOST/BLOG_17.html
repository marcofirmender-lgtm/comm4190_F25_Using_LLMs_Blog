OpenAI’s Circular Money Flow

News
This blog is about how OpenAI continues to fuel its multi-billion dollar rise through funding from competitors.
Author

Marco Firmender

Published

October 26, 2025

I just read the article from The New York Times on how OpenAI is structuring massive fundraising and deal-making efforts, and I found it surprisingly compelling as a lens into the horizon of AI. What struck me was how this isn’t just about one startup getting cash—it’s a glimpse into how the architecture of the entire AI ecosystem is being rewritten.

For instance, OpenAI’s shift toward more flexible capital raising and infrastructure deals signals that training next-gen models isn’t a niche R&D project anymore—it’s a full-on industrial-scale undertaking with huge compute, global partnerships, and commercial stakes. That change aligns directly with what I’ve been thinking about: how AI is moving from “cool prototype” to “core platform for the next decade.” It puts into relief both the enormous opportunity and the risks (financial, governance, safety) that come with it.

I dug into how OpenAI is pulling together its new funding-and-deal structure, and the mechanics are pretty fascinating. According to the article, the model goes beyond just the standard “investors write checks” scenario:

  • OpenAI is raising significant upfront capital from outside investors, but it’s also structuring long-term infrastructure and compute commitments — so investors aren’t just funding operations, they’re underwriting the massive computational backbone needed to train next-level models.
  • There’s a hybrid structure: part equity investment, part deal-making with major tech / cloud partners (e.g., access to specialized hardware, discounted cloud compute) that resembles a strategic partnership more than a pure ownership stake.
  • The company appears to be aligning the incentives such that investors and partners benefit from the model output (e.g., licensing, application deployment) as much as the hardware and compute stepping-stones. In other words: win on the model performance side + get a cut of the commercial rollout.
  • From a governance angle, OpenAI is taking care to set up protective terms around compute access, model safety, and exclusivity for certain partners — implying that the deals aren’t just financial, but also strategic (who gets first access, who holds model-rights, etc.).
  • They’re staggering the funding/deal tranches such that certain milestones (model performance, deployment, revenue generation) unlock further funding or infrastructure commitments. This creates a “checkpoint” structure rather than a lump-sum all upfront.

Putting it into my own framework: instead of thinking of AI as “I need X dollars to train a model,” what OpenAI is doing is more like: “I need a compute-hardware ecosystem + partner alignment + revenue-sharing structure + strategic safeguards.” The mechanics reflect the fact that the scale and cost of training and deploying state-of-the-art AI are so large that simple equity financing isn’t enough; you need structural partnerships and staged funding built into the foundation.

If you like, I can pull out specific numbers and partner names from the article (where available) to map how big the bets are and who gets what.

On a personal level, reading this helped me frame a few of my ongoing interests (in data analysis, market dynamics, tech infrastructure) in a new way. If the AI frontier really is being defined now—not just by better models, but by the corporate-and-capital infrastructure behind them—then understanding the deals, the valuations, and the strategic shifts gives me a better sense of where things are headed. So in short: the article was more than a news item. It was a prompt to rethink where AI is going and how it’s getting there.
